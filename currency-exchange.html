UX Case Study · Nobu Bank

Currency Exchange, built into nobu Go

01Context

Meet Nobu Bank,
and the gap.

Nobu is a growing mid-sized Indonesian bank with 120,000+ users on its mobile app, nobu Go. But one thing was missing. Customers who wanted to buy or hold foreign currency still had to walk into a branch. Competitors had already solved this digitally, and that gap became this project.

02My Role

I took it over mid-project.

I picked up the exchange flow after the lead designer moved to another project. Competitive benchmarking was already done, so my work ran from design exploration all the way through to final handoff.

Key Constraints
Tight timeline, limited resources, and backend dependencies that shaped what we could realistically build.
The Goal
Design an exchange flow that felt simple but stayed transparent. Easy to use, without hiding anything that mattered.
03Landscape

What competitors
were doing.

I reviewed 7 Indonesian banking apps, and one pattern shaped everything: nobody put the full picture together. Both sides of the transaction, a clear live rate, and your account balance, all visible before you even start typing. That was the gap.

CIMB: transfer framing, no rate shown, no account balance, one side at a time.
BLU by BCA: closest structurally, both fields visible, but no balance on the main screen.
myBCA: the most sophisticated rate handling, a 59-second countdown, but only at the confirmation stage.
04Challenge

One screen. A lot to solve.

Currency exchange sounds simple, until you actually design it. Six things had to live on one screen, and every one of them had to be visible, clear, and reliable at the same time. That is what made it harder than it looks.

Two active fields, buy and sell.
The destination account.
The source of fund.
Transaction purpose and additional information.
A compliance disclaimer.
A live rate, with a swap control.
05Explorations

Three explorations.
Each one taught me something.

The strongest part of this project was the messy middle. Each exploration failed in a specific way, and each failure pushed the design closer to something that actually worked.

Exploration 1 · Tabs

Logical on paper. Broken in practice.

The first instinct was tabs: separate buy and sell so users aren't hit with both sides at once. It looked like the right way to reduce load. But building it revealed something the layout couldn't fix. With no source of fund on screen, users had no idea which account the money came from. The rate was there but buried in text, and nothing made it feel live or trustworthy. Three trust problems on one screen. The problem was never focus. It was information.

One side at a time, a buried rate, and a currency selector that read as 'equivalent' rather than a real swap.

Exploration 2 · Too Clean

Clean enough to look good. Not clear enough to work.

The lesson from Exploration 1 was clear: users needed to see their source of fund before committing, so it moved to the main screen. That was the right call. But stripping things back too far created a new problem. The input fields looked like display text, with nothing to signal they were tappable. The rate details hid one tap away behind a bottom sheet. It read as bare rather than intentional. Minimal and clear are not the same thing.

Source of fund came forward, but the fields didn't look interactive and the rate hid behind a sheet.

Exploration 3 · Almost There

The closest one. But two things broke.

Exploration 3 took it further. Source of fund now sat in the same card as the destination account, making the link between account and balance clearer. It was the most complete version yet. But two things still didn't work. The destination account and source of fund looked visually identical, so users had to figure out which was which from context alone. And tabs were still there. When someone asked me to explain the currency dropdown placement and I couldn't, that was the signal. If you can't defend a design choice, it is usually the wrong one.

Better structure, but the two account cards were indistinguishable and the tabs still hadn't earned their place.

06Principle

Don't make users guess
the things that matter.

Across all three explorations, the same problem kept surfacing: users were left to work out the things that mattered most on their own. The final design had one job, to make sure none of these questions ever needed to be asked.

Which account is this coming from?
Is this rate actually live?
Do I even have enough balance?
07Final Design

Everything the user needs.
Nothing they don't.

Tabs are gone. Showing one side at a time was the core problem in Exploration 1, so both sides are visible from the moment the screen opens, and both are editable, because some people start from how much they want to buy and others from how much they want to spend. Currency, account, and balance now sit together as one unit, and the live rate stays visible between the two panels. Everything needed to make a confident decision is right there, before they tap Continue.

Both sides visible and editable from the first second.
Currency, account, and balance grouped as one unit.
Live rate always on screen, with a clear swap control.

From the nobu Go home screen, through Face ID, to a completed exchange.

08Reflection

It wasn't perfect. But it was earned.

Getting two currencies, a live rate, and multiple accounts onto one screen without overwhelming anyone took real iteration. I don't have hard metrics, the feature was new and there were no baselines yet, but the PM's feedback after launch was positive, and there were no significant usability complaints in the first release cycle.

If I went back, one
Real user research. We had competitor analysis and strong business context, but no direct insight into how Nobu's users actually think about foreign currency.
If I went back, two
A rate lock with a countdown timer. We left it out for timeline and backend reasons, but users deserve to know the rate they see is the rate they'll get. It's the biggest gap in this design.
If I went back, three
Usability testing on the swap button. It works, but I'd want real data on whether people actually discover it, or quietly navigate away to start over.

The lesson: complexity isn't something you design away. It's something you design through.
