Improving Hold Cart in a High-Traffic POS

When a cashier puts a transaction on hold, it shouldn't just disappear. Here's how we made it stay visible.

Client
Toko Mama
Role
Sr. Product Designer
Platform
Tablet POS
Constraint
No formal training
01Context02Friction03Strategy04Solution05My Role06Outcome
01Context

A high-traffic store with
no room for confusion.

Toko Mama is a convenience store franchise in Indonesia. It's busy, high-traffic, and built around fast checkout. When the queue is long, every second counts. Interruptions happen constantly; a customer forgets an item, changes their payment method, or steps away mid-transaction. The cashier needs to pause, help someone else, and come back without losing anything.

User Profile
These cashiers aren't navigating software for fun. They're talking to customers, handling cash, and scanning items all at once. The tool had to work with them, not slow them down or make them think twice.
The Business Need
The system needed a simple way to pause a transaction and pick it back up, without losing the cart, without confusion, and without extra steps.
Design Constraint
Training was informal, usually just a manager showing the ropes on day one. There was no onboarding document, no structured walkthrough. Whatever we built had to make sense immediately, without explanation.
02Friction

Saved, but lost.

The Hold Cart feature worked, technically. But the moment a cart was saved, it vanished from the screen. The interface reset to empty. No indicator, no trace. The cashier had confirmed the action, but had no way to see where the cart went.

When the cashier taps "Hold Cart," a snackbar confirms the action for 3 seconds. Then the cart disappears entirely.

The cart disappears from the screen.
The interface resets to an empty state.
There is no visible indicator of where the cart is stored.

Why the Recall button didn't work: there was already a button for this, "Recall Draft." But it had three problems. The label didn't mean much to someone who'd never used the system before. It showed no status, no count, no indication of what was waiting. And most importantly, it only worked if the cashier remembered to look for it. The system was asking them to remember something it should have been showing them.

03Strategy

Direction: make it visible.

The saving action wasn't the problem. The problem was what happened after. Once a cart disappeared from view, the cashier had to rely on memory, and in a busy checkout environment, that's a lot to ask.

The fix wasn't to redesign the feature. It was to stop hiding it. If saved carts stayed visible on screen, cashiers wouldn't need to remember them. They'd just see them.

04Solution

Making saved
carts visible.

When a cart is held, it becomes a tab at the top of the screen. Instead of disappearing, each saved cart remains visible and accessible. White tab means active cart, grey tab means held cart. Cashiers can switch between carts with a single click.

No need to remember saved carts
No searching through hidden menus
Clear overview during peak traffic
05My Role

What I owned, and why.

My Role
Reviewed the initial Hold Cart proposal
Identified usability risks in the interaction flow
Proposed a visibility-based alternative
Refined UI states and edge cases before engineering handoff
Design Considerations
Designed for a maximum of three active hold sessions, aligning with business constraints while preventing interface overload
Ensured intuitive session switching using a familiar tab-based pattern
Maintained operational clarity in high-pressure checkout environments
06Outcome

Clarity over complexity.

By making carts visible, we reduced reliance on memory and minimized hesitation during peak traffic. In a high-pressure retail setting, even seconds matter. So now cashiers can:

See all active sessions at a glance
Switch between carts instantly
Continue transactions without searching

The interaction becomes predictable and easier to operate.

Reflection
Small visibility changes can have a large impact in high-pressure environments. Design decisions should reduce thinking, not add it.
